The Knowledge Quadrant Framework

"The question nobody is asking clearly enough is not whether AI will take your job. It is which part of what you do is actually yours."

The framework maps how people create value at work, and how exposed that value is to AI. Two axes. Knowledge: explicit knowledge is documented and searchable, the kind AI replicates at scale; tacit knowledge is judgment earned by living through real situations. Output: accuracy means there is a right answer and being wrong costs something; originality means there is no right answer, only a fresh one.

AccuracyOriginality
ExplicitTacit

The Doctors

Explicit + Accuracy

Surgeons, lawyers, analysts, engineers writing production code. High stakes, verifiable output. AI is entering this room fastest.

Exposure: highest

The Builders

Explicit + Originality

Marketers, designers, copywriters, product communicators. Pattern-based creativity at scale. AI is displacing the volume tier first.

Exposure: high

The Generals

Tacit + Accuracy

Strategists, senior leaders, negotiators, advisors. Consequential calls on incomplete information. The human premium zone, and it is growing.

Exposure: lower

The Creators

Tacit + Originality

Work that comes from lived judgment and a point of view no one else holds. This quadrant creates history. AI can assist but cannot originate.

Exposure: lowest

Inside the four quadrants live eight personas, from The Doer (highest displacement risk) to The Oracle (minimal). Most people are not where they think they are. I built Kairos to close that gap.

About

I did not set out to become a pricing strategist. I set out to understand how things work, and what they are worth.

Since 2022 I have led pricing strategy at Cohesity through its move to cloud-native SaaS. The Knowledge Quadrant Framework came directly from that work: watching knowledge get commoditized in real time and needing precise language for what was happening.

Before that, Dell was my fifteen-year graduate school. Across India and Round Rock, Texas, I built prediction models, recommendation systems, and pricing architectures under some of the largest commercial decisions the company made. Earlier, Coca-Cola taught me what no engineering curriculum does: the gap between what something costs and what someone pays is a human problem, not a math one.

I started at Infosys at 22, building trade-processing systems for Goldman Sachs, and watched sophisticated work get systematized the moment someone found the repeatable pattern. The offshore model was the original displacement engine. AI is the second one, and I intend to understand it from the inside.

Outside work, I read and write history. I am writing The Arithmetic of Mercy, a novel set in Mauryan India, and I keep returning to Lucknow and Awadh, where so many of my own stories begin.
